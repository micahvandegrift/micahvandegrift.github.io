# Florida's Underground Music Scenes

## Overview

Florida has a distinct personality in the underground. It is a product of several things: geography, demography, and a history of punk that is the same as the national story but that, for some reason, the national story did not acknowledge. This lesson walks through that history and the state's three regional scenes, and ends with a shift in the early 2000s from heavy music toward emo.

## Discussion questions

* Why does Gainesville have such a thriving DIY community? Is it the university?
* How does the influx of people affect the underground? (New ideas, music and movements come in, and a sense of nativism that can mix with underground culture.)
* In a state where scenes are divided by region and not by city, what does "scene" mean?

## Geography and demography

**Geography.** Disconnected from the rest of the country, Florida has historically been left out of certain groupings: it is the southernmost of the contiguous states and yet not always considered part of "the South." It is 361 miles wide and 447 miles long. For the underground, this meant that much of the foundational work done in the 1980s, touring networks most especially, overlooked Florida as a possibility. This changed somewhat through the 1990s, as Gainesville became a punk epicenter and tours started to dip further south.

**Demography.** Florida is typically known for two things, Miami and Disney (the space program may also be included). The ebb and flow of population due to attractions makes Florida a very different state from any other, and the snowbird and tourist market has a profound effect on the state's identity. In the late 2000s it was the nation's third-fastest-growing state, and it has historically grown by outstanding numbers: between 1950 and 1960, the population grew by 78.7 percent. In the late 2000s it was the fourth most populous state, after California, Texas and New York. Most of the population lives along the coast, so the highway becomes the main thoroughfare for travel, and automobile culture is very different from public transportation culture.

The influx of people has a cause-and-effect relationship with the underground. First, it allows new ideas, music and movements to come in. Second, it creates a sense of nativism, which can and does mix with underground culture: the "Florida cracker." Both factors play into the growth and influences of the underground culture.

## A history of Florida punk

The history of Florida punk is the same as national punk, but for some reason Florida was not acknowledged. The story of the scene's history works in conjunction with the regional divisions. The period between 1994 and 2001 was a sort of Renaissance, with many different trends in music reflected across the state.

* **Early to mid-1990s, punk and ska:** Less Than Jake (formed 1992) and Hot Water Music (formed 1994).
* **Mid-1990s, emo and indie:** Peterbuilt, Carlisle, Recess Theory, Divine Child (Denison Mars), Twothirtyeight, and Bagwell and The Miracle Youth.
* **Late 1990s and early 2000s, hardcore:** Poison the Well, Floor, Shai Hulud, Red Roses for a Blue Lady and Dragbody.

## The three regional scenes

The major scenes connect with another state-specific trait: while a specific city is often associated with a scene elsewhere, in Florida scenes are divided into regions.

### North Florida

North Florida takes in Pensacola, Tallahassee, Gainesville and Jacksonville. Gainesville has had the most vibrant and centralizing scene, thanks to its inclusion in the touring networks that came to Athens, Georgia, and even Atlanta. It is home to No Idea Records and Boxcar Records, and Fueled by Ramen was founded there in 1996 (it moved to Tampa after 1998, and to New York in 2007). Gainesville has a thriving DIY community. Why? Is it the university? In the late 2000s there was the Wayward Council (a bookstore, music store and DIY venue), the Civic Media Center (an infoshop and library, founded in 1993), and The Fest.

No Idea is the cornerstone of the Florida underground scene. Its roster includes bands from all over Florida and eventually the U.S. Var Thelin founded it as a zine in 1985. Starting with the sixth issue, the zine came with 7-inch records, the first by a local band called Doldrums and the second a split with Crimpshrine, of Berkeley. The early Gainesville bands mentioned as starting a semblance of a scene are Spoke and Radon, which existed around 1990-1993 and gave rise to the second generation of Gainesville bands. One of them is Hot Water Music: beard punk, gruff vocals, very musically inclined, with an extreme focus on community and making your own way.

### Central Florida

If Gainesville was the head of the Floridian body of music, central Florida was its heart and soul. The melodic strain of Florida music often centralized here. The region includes Melbourne and the beaches, Orlando, Vero Beach and Lakeland. (Tampa and Ocala could fall in here, but their scenes were more aligned with the South Florida hardcore sound.)

Central Florida had a thriving underground Christian music scene for a while, especially in Melbourne: Dear Ephesus and Once Fallen. The Tab was a church that allowed punk shows and was organized by a body of locals who came to be called the Little Reggies, set up very much like 924 Gilman.

Orlando, as the center of central Florida, has long been touted as the center of the underground. In the late 2000s, the depth and history of the Orlando scene were still being uncovered. In the mid-1990s it was centered on Sapphire Supper Club (now The Social) and Park Ave CDs, in Winter Park. As tours started coming to Florida, they would end up at Sapphire or at Club Firestone, in the heart of downtown Orlando on Orange Avenue. Sapphire was *the* place where many cross-state relationships were formed, fostered and continued. There was also a little record store called DIY, near UCF, founded in 1997, which carried a large collection of vinyl and hosted shows in a small area in the back. Jeff Hogan and Anthony Torres put together a compilation of local Central Florida bands, *Spirit of '99: A Hardcore Competition*. Famous Orlando bands include Peterbuilt, whose members went on to My Hotel Year, Carlisle, Legends of Rodeo (Lake Worth) and The Miracle Youth.

Lakeland also had an important influence. It supported a smaller scene centered on house shows and The Mad Hatter, and produced a few bands in the screamo genre (Cowboys Become Folk Heroes) and SaGoh 24/7, later Anberlin (from Winter Haven).

### South Florida

South Florida takes in Pompano Beach, West Palm Beach, Miami and Fort Lauderdale. It is most often associated with South Florida hardcore, and also with New Found Glory and Dashboard Confessional. This is a different brand of hardcore from what we have discussed so far: hardcore concerned with melody, with riffs similar to metal, and very musically technical. Bands of note are Strongarm, Shai Hulud, The Young and the Useless, Poison the Well and Floor. There is no real center of activity; it is a wide genre with an expansive geographic reach.

Respectable Street Café (nicknamed "Respectables") in West Palm Beach has hosted many major bands from Florida and the underground since it opened in 1987. It was founded by Rodney Mayo.

South Florida also represents an important shift in Florida music. When Strongarm broke up, the remaining members formed a band called Further Seems Forever. This band and its style were caught in the midst of the emo craze of the early 2000s. Then its lead singer, Chris Carrabba, went on to Dashboard Confessional, which became for many the face of emo in America. The shift from heavy music to emo-inspired music resonated across the state at around the same time, and Underoath is a perfect example. Some did not make the shift: in the late 2000s the Tampa scene was still essentially heavy, and there were scenes that hosted lots of heavy bands, but for a while it seemed that the emo movement was overtaking Florida.
