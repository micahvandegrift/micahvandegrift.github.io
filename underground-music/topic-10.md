# New Scenes, Labels, Clubs and Sounds

## Overview

This topic covers the mid-to-late 1990s transitions in indie, emo and the underground: the labels that grew up around the music, and the clubs and scenes. The point of looking at a case study like Chicago, and of watching a film about Saddle Creek, is to see how scenes, especially in mid-1990s emo, are incestuous. The mainstream is constantly trying to put everything in one category, while a close look shows that regions still maintained specific sounds.

## Discussion questions

**On labels**

* Vagrant complicates the model of record labels from the 1980s. For many it represents a drastic shift in business practices. Do we agree? Had this shift been coming for a while?
* What was the disagreement between Vagrant and Alkaline Trio?

**On the Kinsella interview**

* The way these brothers think and talk about music is new and different. How?
* What do they say about politics? What is the "collective consciousness" of technology, and how does it affect the masses? Does it undo the Enlightenment?
* They think about music in terms of philosophical ideas and enlightenment of the collective unconscious. What does this represent for the underground? Intellectual versus aggressive. Is this intellectualized approach different from Sonic Youth, Fugazi, Pavement and Guided by Voices?
* How is their inspiration for songs new or different for the underground? Compare the sound of a rock band, which everyone knows, with twisting that sound to make people think outside the norm. Is this artistic abstraction related to Sunny Day Real Estate?
* They talk about the structure of songwriting as split between the members. Is this dichotomy of melodic versus chaotic indicative of the 1990s underground as a whole? Is music a means rather than an end? Emo dynamics of loud/soft equal melodic/chaotic?
* They place emphasis on the process of music as opposed to the product, which continually separates this band, and in a way the new underground, from the mainstream. What about artistic intent? The intentional fallacy and the affective fallacy? In literary criticism, W.K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley coined the first term (1946) for the mistake of judging a work by what its author meant, and the second (1949) for the mistake of judging it by its emotional effect on readers.
* What about the morality or ethics of being independent? Does it change for these musicians? How does Fugazi's influence show?
* Tim says they are "always conscious of leaving space for the listeners, and the song is not completed until someone hears it." What does he mean? He wants to activate people, to be a conductor, like an electrical circuit, and says the point is not to wake up the masses but at least to keep himself awake. Compare this with Ted Leo's essay: is there a responsibility for the audience in discriminating about the art you support? How does that work for emo culture?

## The labels

### Jade Tree (Wilmington, Delaware)

Darren Walters and Tim Owen started Jade Tree in 1990; its first release came in August 1991. Both started out with straight edge hardcore labels, and as the genre faded they went in on Jade Tree, specializing at first in post-hardcore and noise rock, then in emo and melodic hardcore. In 1995 they signed Lifetime and put out *Hello Bastards*, an East Coast version of Jawbreaker. In 1996 they released the Promise Ring's *30 Degrees Everywhere*, and in 1997 *Nothing Feels Good*; *30 Degrees* sold 4,000 copies on the road before it was even out. The Promise Ring formed in Milwaukee in 1995 around Davey von Bohlen, who left Cap'n Jazz to focus on it. Other bands include Jets to Brazil, Pedro the Lion, Texas Is the Reason and The Blood Brothers.

### Vagrant (Santa Monica)

Rich Egan and Jon Cohen founded Vagrant in 1995 out of Egan's management deal with Face to Face. The first release was a compilation called *West by North-South*. Then came the *Before You Were Punk* compilation, featuring pop punk bands playing covers of 1980s new wave hits, which sold more than 70,000 copies. It caught the attention of The Get Up Kids, from the Kansas City area, whose Doghouse debut, *Four Minute Mile*, was widely popular. Vagrant released *Something to Write Home About* in 1999, caught the new emo buzz, and marketed it to the new fans. Other bands include Alkaline Trio, Dashboard Confessional, Saves the Day and Thrice.

### No Idea (Gainesville, Florida)

Var Thelin founded No Idea as a zine in 1985. With his sixth issue, he put out a 7-inch, the first by the local Gainesville band Doldrums. The bands associated with No Idea span the entire country and various genres: Atom and His Package, Assholeparade, Assuck, I Hate Myself, Planes Mistaken for Stars, Less Than Jake. It is seen as a major contributor to the American punk community and has put out records by more than a few bands labeled "beard punk," most notably Hot Water Music and Against Me. It is tied to The Fest, the Gainesville festival that showcases bands from all over. The first Fest was held in May 2002, and it has been held in the fall every year since 2003.

### Polyvinyl (Illinois)

Polyvinyl began as a fanzine called the *Polyvinyl Press*, which Matt Lunsford and Darcie Knight ran in Danville, Illinois, while they were still in high school; it became a record label in 1996 and moved to Champaign in 2001. They were tight with members of Braid, a quartet from Champaign-Urbana, who convinced them to release their 7-inch single. The roster includes American Football, Rainer Maria, Mates of State and Aloha.

### Doghouse (Toledo, Ohio)

Dirk Hemsath founded Doghouse in Toledo in late 1987, originally to release his own band's material, Majority of One. It is known best as a jumping-off point for other indie labels: The Get Up Kids, Hot Water Music and As Friends Rust all started there. In 2002 it released The All-American Rejects, and from 2005 it was distributed by WEA, which made it an affiliate of Warner Music Group.

### Crank

Jeff Matlow founded Crank in September 1994, in Santa Monica, California. It is known for putting out records by some early Midwest emo bands: Mineral, Boys Life, The Gloria Record and Cursive (their debut album).

### Deep Elm (Charlotte, North Carolina)

John Szuch founded Deep Elm in 1995. It is known mainly for its *Emo Diaries* compilations, the first of which appeared in 1997, and its *This Is Indie Rock* series, which collect new and unreleased songs by young bands from around the world. By the label's own count they introduced more than 135 bands, among them Jimmy Eat World, Appleseed Cast, Further Seems Forever and Samiam. Bands were selected from open submissions, and the label has said it never tried to define any musical style. It helped launch bands like Appleseed Cast, Benton Falls and The White Octave.

### Saddle Creek

Saddle Creek is covered in the film *Spend an Evening with Saddle Creek*. The label is based in Omaha, Nebraska, and its artists include Bright Eyes, Cursive and The Faint.

## Anatomy of a scene: Chicago

Our case study is the Chicago family of bands: Cap'n Jazz, Joan of Arc, Owls, Make Believe and Owen.

Cap'n Jazz formed in 1989 in Buffalo Grove, Illinois, outside Chicago, with the brothers Mike and Tim Kinsella, Sam Zurick and Victor Villarreal, and later added Davey von Bohlen. They stayed mainly in the Midwest and released several EPs and songs on compilations. They are known for Tim Kinsella's strange vocal style and lyrics. They put out *Shmap'n Shmazz*, their only full-length album, in 1995 on Man with Gun.

After that album they went in different directions. Davey went to the Promise Ring. Tim founded Joan of Arc, sometimes including Mike and Sam Zurick. Mike did solo projects, American Football and later Owen. Victor Villarreal played in Ghosts and Vodka. During Joan of Arc's temporary breakup in 2001, Tim reassembled the members of Cap'n Jazz, minus Davey, into the band Owls. In 2003, Tim, his cousin Nate Kinsella, Sam Zurick and a friend, Bobby Burg, started Make Believe, which began as a touring version of Joan of Arc. In 1998 Jade Tree released nearly all of Cap'n Jazz's recordings in a two-disc set called *Analphabetapolothology*.

Joan of Arc's website describes how they dealt with the exposure the anthology brought. A group of teenage friends who had played in a punk band was suddenly being described in zines as having galvanized a genre, as having dominated the Chicago indie scene of the early 1990s, and as having helped move emo from a deeply underground punk subgenre toward a more widely accepted part of indie rock. According to the site, those claims were false: the band's following had been highly regional and by no means dominant, and its influence was probably being exaggerated because of the success of Davey von Bohlen's band, The Promise Ring. And their later band was now tagged with a genre buzzword that didn't fit.
