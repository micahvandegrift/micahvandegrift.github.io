# No Wave and Sonic Youth

*Topic 2 of the course archive.*

## Overview

This topic starts in New York with No Wave, an offshoot of late-1970s punk that rejected melody and technique, and then follows Sonic Youth, a band that came out of punk, mixed it with No Wave and tied it to experimental music. Formed in New York in 1981, they went from dissonant tunings and "prepared" instruments to a major label deal, and one of their albums, *Daydream Nation*, was later added to the Library of Congress's National Recording Registry.

Sonic Youth are a case study in what the underground could do. They asked to be taken seriously as art, reached back before punk for influence, treated relationships and networking as currency, and brought the idea of "negation" back into rock. In the words of the reading, they were "more an inspiration than an influence."

## No Wave and *No New York*

No Wave was an offshoot of late-1970s punk, concentrated mainly in New York, from 1978 to 1982. The name played off the New Wave bands of the day: The B-52's, The Cars, Devo, Talking Heads. The fashion was choppy haircuts and skinny suits and ties.

It was different from punk. No Wave rejected melody as well as technique, and it put musical texture ahead of tone or style. It was atonal and dissonant. It was as much performance art as music, and it was iconoclastic, all about tearing down the foundations of music.

Brian Eno put together *No New York* (1978) to document the scene. He heard the scene at an Artists Space festival of ten local bands in early 1978 and recorded four of them, four songs each: The Contortions, Teenage Jesus and the Jerks, Mars and DNA.

## Sonic Youth

Sonic Youth's core lineup from 1985 on was Thurston Moore, Kim Gordon, Lee Ranaldo and Steve Shelley. Other members over the years include Richard Edson (the first drummer), Bob Bert, Jim Sclavunos and Jim O'Rourke. They formed in 1981 in New York City.

They came out of punk, mixed it with No Wave and tied it to experimental music. They named their influences as Glenn Branca, The Stooges, The Velvet Underground, Black Sabbath, MC5 and the Grateful Dead. They were also deeply affected by seeing Minor Threat perform in May 1982 (Azerrad, p. 238).

Their sound came from a variety of different tunings and from "prepared" instruments, much like the experiments of Henry Cowell and John Cage. They specialized in dissonant tones, so a quick music theory review of consonance versus dissonance helps here.

### The story

In December 1981 they recorded an EP for Glenn Branca's Neutral Records. In November 1982 they toured with the Swans, on what was called the Savage Blunder Tour. In 1983 the band released *Confusion Is Sex*, with Jim Sclavunos on drums; Bob Bert returned later that year. A two-week European tour that summer was well received. Moore began publishing a fanzine called *Killer*, and interviewed Ian MacKaye in the first issue.

In 1984, a disastrous show in London led to good press in the English magazines *Sounds* and *NME*. Their next album, *Bad Moon Rising* (1985), commented on American culture at the time, and negation was part of it.

In 1985 they made their first music video, for "Death Valley '69," directed by Richard Kern. The song, a collaboration with Lydia Lunch, was inspired by the Manson murders, and the video is violent and grisly. Bob Bert, who had just left the band, and Steve Shelley, the new drummer, both appear in it. In January 1985 they played in Seattle and met Bruce Pavitt, who paid $100 to use "Kill Yr. Idols" on a compilation called *Sub Pop 100*, which came out in 1986.

In early 1986 they signed to SST Records (Greg Ginn of Black Flag's label) and recorded *EVOL* in March. It is an album obsessed with celebrity, and it got good press in the U.S., in *People* and the *New York Times*. It was an artistic breakthrough for the band (Azerrad, p. 263).

In 1987 came *Sister*, partly inspired by the life and works of Philip K. Dick. It sold 60,000 copies, and critics voted it the twelfth-best album of 1987 in the *Village Voice* Pazz & Jop poll.

In 1988 they released the double LP *Daydream Nation* on Enigma, distributed by Capitol, which was partly owned by EMI. CMJ, *Rolling Stone* and others lauded it as one of the best albums of the year. Distribution problems arose, and the band began to look for a major label deal. Its opening track, "Teen Age Riot," is a pivotal song in their career.

In 1990 they released *Goo* on Geffen Records, with the single "Kool Thing," featuring Chuck D as a guest. It is considered a more accessible album. In 1991 they toured Europe with Nirvana and other bands, which was documented in *1991: The Year Punk Broke* (released in 1992). In 1992 they released *Dirty* on DGC; the video for "100%" was by the newly discovered Spike Jonze and featured Jason Lee skateboarding.

*Experimental Jet Set, Trash and No Star* followed in 1994 on DGC, with "Bull in the Heather" as a pseudo-hit (it reached No. 13 on the Modern Rock chart). There was no full tour for it, because Kim Gordon was pregnant. In 1995 they headlined Lollapalooza, and in 1996 they appeared on *The Simpsons*, in the episode "Homerpalooza."

They began releasing albums on their own label, SYR Records, based in Hoboken, New Jersey. *SYR3* was the first to feature Jim O'Rourke, who later became a full member. *SYR4*, released in 1999, is made up of covers of avant-garde composers: John Cage, Steve Reich and Yoko Ono. Later albums include *NYC Ghosts and Flowers* (2000), *Murray Street* (2002), *Sonic Nurse* (2004) and *Rather Ripped* (2006).

In April 2006 the Library of Congress announced that *Daydream Nation* had been added to the National Recording Registry, in the class of 2005.

## What they brought to the underground

These points come from Azerrad's chapter on the band.

**Timbre, texture and rhythm.** Sonic Youth put a premium on timbre, harmonic texture and rhythm. Timbre is the character or quality of a musical sound as distinct from its pitch and intensity.

**Art.** They asked to be taken seriously as art. They bridged the worlds of high art and low art: like Warhol, they mixed pop culture with high art. Arty versus edgy (p. 264).

**Nostalgia.** They allowed their music to be nostalgic, reaching back before punk for influence.

**Relationships as currency.** Empowerment came through grassroots connections, as opposed to the mainstream, where bands are marketed into existence. They recognized the importance of networking and put it into practice, carrying many other bands to notoriety alongside them (pp. 254-256).

**A framework for indie rock.** They carried the music from punk's working-class roots to urban high art, and pioneered the search for new sounds in rock (p. 243).

**Negation.** They brought the concept of "negation" back into rock (p. 249). Negation is the refusal to ignore harsh realities: the world is not as it seems. Greil Marcus is credited with the term, in an *Artforum* piece on Sonic Youth. It had been used by 1960s bands such as CCR, MC5, The Stooges and The Velvet Underground, and it was a reaction against Tom Petty, John Mellencamp and Bruce Springsteen.

**Physical versus cerebral.** This is the line drawn between hardcore and pre-indie, for instance Minor Threat compared with The Flaming Lips. It plays into underground music for the rest of its life.

**Inspiration more than influence.** In Azerrad's phrase, they were more an inspiration than an influence (p. 273).
