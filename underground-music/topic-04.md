# Scenes and Labels

## Overview

Often there is a specific sound associated with a scene. This topic asks how a scene differs from a subculture, and how we construct scene identity as opposed to subcultural identity. A scene can be talked about as a collective of people, as an ideal, or as a fashion and marketing statement, and it is worth asking whether any one of these is more authentic than the others.

We look at the major American scenes and record labels of the 1980s: Dischord in Washington, DC; SST in Southern California; Touch and Go in Michigan and Chicago; Sub Pop in Seattle; Kill Rock Stars and K Records in the Pacific Northwest; and Mordam, the distribution company that tied many of them together. Then we look closely at one scene, Berkeley, California, through the all-ages club 924 Gilman and two of its bands, Fifteen and Operation Ivy.

## What a scene is made of

A scene is made up of bands and music, a sense of community, a venue, a record store, and a zine or label. A punk scene is local, and it can be a handful of friends or thousands of people. Most have the same parts: bands and the fans who come to see them, places to play, small labels that press the records, and people who make zines, posters and clothes. A small core of committed people sits in the middle, with a looser crowd around the edges.

## The labels

### Dischord (Washington, DC)

Dischord was started by Jeff Nelson and Ian MacKaye after the breakup of the Teen Idles. Its first release, in December 1980, was the Teen Idles' *Minor Disturbance* EP. S.O.A. followed in early 1981, and by the end of that year the label had also put out singles by Minor Threat, Government Issue and Youth Brigade. In October 1981 the label's operations moved to a house in Arlington, Virginia, known as the Dischord House, while its mailing address stayed on Beecher Street in Washington, DC.

Dischord put out its first record in December 1980. On its website the label says it releases only music from the Washington, DC scene, and that "We do not work with contracts so our relationship with our bands is based on friendship and trust." Notable bands include Beefeater, Fugazi, Jawbox and Q and Not U. Dischord created the model of independent record labels that many others followed.

### SST (Southern California)

SST was run by Greg Ginn of Black Flag. It was formed in 1978 in Long Beach, California, growing out of a mail-order electronics business Ginn had run since he was twelve, whose name is given in sources as either "Solid State Transmitters" or "Solid State Tuners." Its first record, in January 1979, was Black Flag's *Nervous Breakdown* EP. It became a prominent figure in L.A. punk, releasing the Descendents, the Minutemen and many more. From 1982, with Hüsker Dü, it began working with bands from outside Southern California, and Sonic Youth followed in 1986.

SST differed from labels like Touch and Go in several ways, as Dave Lang lays out in his history of the label for *Perfect Sound Forever*. It did not mind being unfashionable, its owners were somewhat older than the typical punk, its roster was more eclectic, and it would not tie its bands to any larger "movement."

Notable bands include Black Flag, Dinosaur Jr., Hüsker Dü, the Meat Puppets and Soundgarden. Mike Watt, of the Minutemen, Stooges and fIREHOSE, said of Dinosaur Jr.: "Being an SST band had nothing to do with the sound, it just meant you knew there were going to be some weirdos involved." Several bands that left SST complained about payments, distribution or the handling of their records, among them Hüsker Dü, the Meat Puppets and Sonic Youth, and some fought to reclaim their masters. J Mascis regained the masters to Dinosaur Jr.'s first three albums from SST in 2004. Spot was the engineer on many SST releases.

### Touch and Go (Michigan, then Chicago)

Touch and Go began in 1979 as a fanzine started in East Lansing, Michigan, by Tesco Vee and Dave Stimson. It became a record label in 1981, and its first record was a 7-inch EP by the Necros, *Sex Drive*. Corey Rusk, the Necros' bassist, took over the label in 1983 when Vee moved to Washington, DC. It concentrated on hardcore bands from Michigan and Ohio: the Necros, the Meatmen and Negative Approach. In the mid-1980s it added Big Black and the Butthole Surfers. The Rusks later moved the label to Chicago, and it released records by Slint, The Jesus Lizard, the Butthole Surfers and more.

Touch and Go was known for fair business practices: 50/50 deals, in which half the profits go to the artists after the label recoups promotion and production costs, and oral contracts, "handshake deals." It did all its own pressing and distribution. In 1990 it began manufacturing and distributing for other labels, including KRS, Thrill Jockey, Merge and Le Tigre. Graystone Hall in Detroit hosted hardcore bands in the early 1980s, and from 1986 Corey Rusk managed it as the city's main all-ages punk venue. Russ Gibb, who had run the Grande Ballroom in the 1960s, where the MC5 and The Stooges played, is said to have funded it. Notable bands include Big Black, Die Kreuzen, Pinback, Ted Leo and !!!.

### Sub Pop (Seattle)

Sub Pop began as a fanzine called *Subterranean Pop*, started by Bruce Pavitt in Olympia, Washington, in the early 1980s while he was a student at Evergreen State College, and became a Seattle label in 1986, with the *Sub Pop 100* compilation. Its early releases were Soundgarden and Mudhoney, and it was looked at as the grunge label. Later signings included Sunny Day Real Estate, Sebadoh, Iron and Wine, The Postal Service, The Shins, Holopaw and The Album Leaf. In 1995 the label's owners sold 49 percent of it to Warner Music Group.

Its platinum records include Nirvana's *Bleach* and The Postal Service's *Give Up*, and The Shins' "New Slang" went gold as a digital download. Holopaw, from Gainesville, Florida, released a self-titled EP in 2003, after their singer collaborated on Ugly Casanova's *Sharpen Your Teeth* with Isaac Brock of Modest Mouse. Notable bands also include Beat Happening, Mudhoney, Modest Mouse and Iron and Wine.

### Kill Rock Stars (Seattle and Olympia)

Kill Rock Stars was founded in 1991 by Slim Moon. Its first release was a split 7-inch of spoken-word pieces by Slim Moon and Kathleen Hanna. A compilation followed featuring Bikini Kill, the Melvins and Bratmobile, and the label is most notable for its riot grrrl releases. In 1995 it released Elliott Smith's self-titled album, in 1997 Sleater-Kinney, and later The Decemberists and The Gossip. Slim Moon left in 2006 to work for Nonesuch Records, and his wife, Portia Sabin, took over the label. Notable bands include Bikini Kill, The Decemberists, Elliott Smith, Team Dresch and Br. Danielson.

### K Records (Olympia, Washington)

K Records was founded in 1982 in Olympia by Calvin Johnson, who had worked at Sub Pop with Pavitt. It was not strictly cassette-only, but most of its early releases were cassettes, beginning with tapes by the Olympia band the Supreme Cool Beings; its first vinyl record was a Beat Happening single in 1984. It is the home of Beat Happening and Dub Narcotic, and groups that have worked with K include Built to Spill, Little Wings, The Make-Up, Mirah and Modest Mouse. Its motto is "exploding the teenage underground into passionate revolt against the corporate ogre since 1982." The label has been so influential in anti-corporate independent music and DIY punk culture, particularly in Olympia, that it was the subject of a 2000 documentary by Heather Rose Dominic, *The Shield Around the K*.

In 1987 K started a series of 7-inch records called "International Pop Underground," featuring underground artists from around the globe. Beat Happening are the foundation of the "twee pop" or "lo-fi" genre. Little Wings is Kyle Field.

### Mordam Records

Mordam was a distribution company for independent labels, founded in San Francisco in 1982 by Ruth Schwartz, a co-editor of *Maximum Rocknroll*. It was known for paying the labels it distributed on time, and its roster included Alternative Tentacles, Lookout, Flipside and Kill Rock Stars.

## Case study: 924 Gilman

924 Gilman Street in Berkeley opened with its first show on December 31, 1986. Tim Yohannan of *Maximum Rocknroll* was one of its founders. It runs on a strict DIY code of ethics. All ages is one of its basic concerns, because there were so few all-ages venues in Northern California. There are three basic rules: no violence, no drugs, no alcohol. Patrons buy a membership card, which gives them a sense of ownership. The club is entirely volunteer-run, and membership meetings on the first and third Saturday of every month discuss issues, vote on them and make them law.

Bands must send their lyrics before playing there, and racist, homophobic and misogynistic bands do not play there. The club's policy is no major-label bands. Green Day were barred after signing with Reprise Records in 1994, though they have played there since with the membership's approval, and AFI may play only when the membership approves a particular show. The music is mostly pop punk and hardcore, and past bands include Operation Ivy, Green Day, Jawbreaker, the Mr. T Experience, Samiam and more. Money is split half and half between the band and the club. The club is also used by political groups and for art shows and films.

Max Raynard says, "DIY is about empowerment." That is part of how Gilman works, by giving power to the people.

## The Berkeley scene

### Lookout Records

Larry Livermore and David Hayes founded Lookout Records in 1987. It specialized in punk, pop punk and hardcore, and in the late 1980s and early 1990s the Lookout name would sell records on its reputation. Its bands included Crimpshrine, Fifteen, Isocracy, the Mr. T Experience, Operation Ivy, Samiam and Screeching Weasel. In 2005 the label ran into trouble with former bands over unpaid royalties, and Green Day, Operation Ivy, Avail, Screeching Weasel and others took back the rights to their recordings.

### *Maximum Rocknroll*

*Maximum Rocknroll* is possibly the most widely recognized fanzine of the punk subculture. Its coverage is wide-ranging, and it has lasted as the scene has changed. Tim Yohannan founded it in 1982 as a newsprint insert in the compilation *Not So Quiet on the Western Front* (Alternative Tentacles). It has a strong focus on international scenes, it is all-volunteer, non-profit and strictly independent, and it will not interview bands that are on or distributed by major labels.

### Fifteen

Fifteen's lineup changed often. Jeff Ott was the one constant, and the players over the years included John Ogle, Chris Flanagan and Mark Moreno. Ott and Aaron Cometbus had both come from Crimpshrine, one of the most important Northern California bands of the 1980s; Ott went on to front Fifteen, and Cometbus did not play in it. Their subject matter typically includes homelessness, drug abuse, needle exchange, environmental destruction and the government's use of counter-intelligence measures to violate activists' civil rights. Lookout released their first full-length album, *Swain's First Bike Ride*, in 1991. *Lucky* (1999, Sub City Records) is their most influential album: very political and to the point.

### Operation Ivy

Operation Ivy were Jesse Michaels, Tim Armstrong, Matt Freeman and Dave Mello. They played ska-punk from 1987 to 1989. They debuted in October 1987 on a *Maximum Rocknroll* compilation, *Turn It Around!*, and in January 1988 signed with Lookout, which put out the *Hectic* EP that year. There was pressure to sign with a major label, because the genre was growing in popularity at the time. Lookout put out *Energy* in 1989, and their last show was on May 28, 1989, at 924 Gilman, which was also Green Day's first show under that name. In all, they played 185 shows and recorded 32 songs. Their songs are about wanting a fairer world and about refusing to go along with mainstream, conformist culture. Hellcat Records, a division of Epitaph, reissued *Energy* in 2004.
