# Emo

## Overview

What is emo? It can be seen as the culmination of many trends and musical styles from the 1980s and 1990s. The fragmentation of the subculture, due to changing conceptions of what is and isn't acceptable, accounts for the difficulty of explaining and defining emo culture. Over the course of the 1990s, and especially into the 2000s, emo changed hands from one generation to the next, from the underground to the mainstream, and from negative connotations to positive reinforcement.

This lesson asks what emo is as a history, a genre and a subculture, then looks at how technology changed the underground, from tapes to CDs and MP3s and from zines to magazines and blogs, and closes with two bands often pointed to as the ones that brought the "down to earth" emotional into emo: Jawbreaker and Sunny Day Real Estate.

## What is emo?

The Warped Tour began in June 1995. It became the breeding ground for a new culture of bands aimed at a teenage audience. Many of the early tours were headlined by second-wave punk bands, but by the late 2000s the trend had swung toward "new emo."

### Emo is constantly changing: three waves

* **First wave (1980s): emocore.** Rites of Spring. A reaction against hardcore: melodic but still very raw.
* **Second wave (1990s): indie emo.** Jimmy Eat World, The Get Up Kids, Braid, The Promise Ring, Texas Is the Reason, Mineral. Slowed down, a mix of indie rock's musical sense (it sounded more like music) with personal and sometimes nonsensical lyrics. "Red and Blue Jeans" and "Wakingtowinter" are examples.
* **Third wave (2000s): pop emo.** Thursday, Dashboard Confessional, Jimmy Eat World (*Bleed American*), Taking Back Sunday. "Emo in the arena!" Big sounds, powerful, catchy tunes. Associations with emo are more in the culture than in the history, music or themes. "Again I Go Unnoticed" and "Sweetness" are examples.

### Emo as a genre

Emo is characterized by softer, more aesthetically pleasing sounds than hardcore, more structured than experimental music, and more melodic than grunge. Often the themes are about love, relationships or growing up. As the first and second generations grew up, the music splintered into subgenres, which began to appeal to new audiences through the portal of emo: indie, hardcore (Shai Hulud), screamo (Reversal of Man), neo-folk (Iron and Wine), post-rock (Explosions in the Sky) and metalcore (Zao). The splintering of the scene comes with the invasion of pop culture.

### Emo as a subculture

Emo has all the attributes of a subculture. In the tradition of tongue-in-cheek how-to guides, there is a specific fashion (*Everybody Hurts: An Essential Guide to Emo Culture*), a centralizing ideal (the music), and a connection with youth: Greenwald constantly reminds us in his book that these are kids, teens, adolescents. It interacts with the mainstream culture. This comes out of the tradition that early indie and grunge paved, and the subculture works as an extension of the mainstream culture.

## Technology and communication: tapes to CDs, zines to blogs

Technology changed the subculture in two ways: communication, and the business of music, in both the underground and the mainstream. The music business shifted from album-generated sales to singles, and back. The rise of technology, especially the MP3, revolutionized the music industry, and it is still adapting.

### The MP3

* MPEG audio files, first in the MP2 format and later in MP3, began to appear on the internet in the early 1990s, and MP3 files spread widely in the second half of the decade.
* The Internet Underground Music Archive was created by three people at UC Santa Cruz in 1993, to help unsigned artists release their music and gain a wide audience, circumventing the music industry's model of distribution through a record label. It was a pioneer of internet use for music and popularized independent use of technology to spread music.
* The Fraunhofer Society, a German research organization, developed the MP3 compression format, with Karlheinz Brandenburg leading the work. Winplay3, an early MP3 player for personal computers, was released on September 9, 1995. Winamp (1997) and Napster (1999) furthered the rise of the MP3 and the changing face of the music industry.

### The information age and the underground

The teens who grew up alongside 1990s emo, indie and underground music were among the first to grow up online. The subculture changed in three basic ways. It went from regional to national, because the internet let the Midwest interact with the Northeast in seconds. Its media got an updated look: photocopied zines became glossy magazines. And the way of experiencing the subculture changed.

The online social community is tied inextricably to the growth and popularity of indie culture: Friendster, Makeoutclub, MySpace. This is a move from real community to imagined community. And don't believe the hype: print media is not dead, it is just underground. The technological advances of the mid-1990s changed forever the way the subculture is disseminated, and the exponential speeding up of our culture has changed the landscape. The mixtape, a form of speech particular to the late twentieth century, is being replaced by the playlist.

### From zine to magazine

A zine (short for "magazine" or "fanzine") is a noncommercial, often homemade publication, usually made in small numbers by people who write it and hand it out or trade it themselves.

* ***Cometbus.*** Begun in Berkeley in 1981 and big in the late 1980s, handwritten by Aaron Cometbus, a punk musician from the East Bay (Crimpshrine).
* ***No Answers.*** Written by Kent McClard, who later went on to publish *HeartattaCk*. Begun in the mid-1980s, it was often poetry and photocopied pictures of bands. Its ninth issue came with a 7-inch by Downcast, which became Ebullition #1, the beginning of a record label that put out records by Ampere, Los Crudos and Reversal of Man.
* ***HeartattaCk.*** March 1994 to June 2006. It would not review anything with a UPC label. After it ended, several contributors went on to found Give Me Back.
* ***Anti-Matter.*** Published by Norman Brannon (also known as Norm Arenas) in the mid-1990s, a little more professional and intellectual. Brannon was also a founding member and guitarist of Texas Is the Reason.
* ***Law of Inertia.*** Ross Siegel, at Cornell. This is seen as one of the major moves from zine to magazine: full-color pages, actual interviews with big bands.
* ***Alternative Press.*** Mike Shea, Cleveland, mid-1980s. Its first issue's front-page editorial was about the Smiths not playing Cleveland. For a few years it was black and white, with interviews with local acts and an occasional large group. It went through a nu metal phase, with covers devoted to Korn, Limp Bizkit and Insane Clown Posse, who was on eight covers. Then it caught on to the early emo bands before it became a buzzword, with Saves the Day, Dashboard Confessional and Thursday all on covers. The magazine grew with the subculture and, by the late 2000s, was widely read as a kind of "new emo" bible.

## Jawbreaker and Sunny Day Real Estate

An important factor starts to come out of mid-1990s emo bands: they burn out quickly. Why? These two bands are pointed to as the ones that really brought the "down to earth" emotional into emo. The highlight of both, and one that becomes an emo trait, is the connection the audience feels to the band, and burning out quickly because of attention, media and culture. 1994 was the year for both.

### Jawbreaker

Jawbreaker were Blake Schwarzenbach, Chris Bauermeister and Adam Pfahler. They formed at NYU in 1986, spent a few years in Los Angeles and relocated to San Francisco in 1991. In January 1990 they recorded *Unfun*, which came out that May, followed by a ten-week tour and then a breakup while the members went back to school. *Bivouac* came out in December 1992. Andy Greenwald wrote that its lyrics were taken from Blake's diaries, and critics heard it as slower, heavier and gloomier than *Unfun*. In a sense, the album was a rejection of the fast hardcore of *Unfun*.

In 1992, after the "Chesterfield King" single, they went on the ten-week "Hell Is on the Way" tour of the Midwest and East Coast, then to Europe. Over the long tour, Blake's raspy singing caught up with him, and he needed throat surgery in London in October to remove a polyp.

*24 Hour Revenge Therapy* (early 1994, Tupelo/Communion Records) was, in Rich Egan's words, "just 11 cut and dry little stories about living in the East Bay… straight and to the point." The bulk of it was recorded in Chicago in May 1993 with Steve Albini, who preferred not to be credited. The band toured the U.S. on the "When It Pains, It Roars" tour through July, recorded three more songs in San Francisco in August 1993, and in October played six dates with Nirvana.

"Indictment" is a song Blake introduced as a "Scathing Indictment of the Pop Industry" in some 1992 and 1993 performances. In an early 1992 version he sang different words than on the record, and in some 1996 shows he changed "Will they ever learn?" to "Will we ever learn?" Is this a challenge to the connection between audience and artist?

### Sunny Day Real Estate

Sunny Day Real Estate were Jeremy Enigk, Dan Hoerner, William Goldsmith and Nate Mendel. They were fans of punk, but all accomplished musicians with high ambitions. They existed as a four-piece only briefly before releasing their debut album, and released several 7-inches and EPs before *Diary*. The group was shrouded in mystery from the start: they released only one picture to the press, conducted one interview, and, for some still-unknown reason, never played a show in California with all four members.

*Diary* (1994, Sub Pop) became one of Sub Pop's best sellers, with more than 231,000 copies sold by 2008. It has extremely poetic lyrics, and it was the album that made emo accessible. In September 1994 they played "Seven" on *The Jon Stewart Show*, and a few days later recorded several songs for MTV's *120 Minutes*. The sounds they achieved on this album set a specific way of doing emo, and its loud/soft dynamic was reproduced by many groups after them.

After this album the band announced its breakup in early 1995; rumor blamed Jeremy's conversion to Christianity, a link he later downplayed. The rhythm section, Nate Mendel and William Goldsmith, went on to play with the Foo Fighters. The band reunited in 1997 but never achieved the same status.
