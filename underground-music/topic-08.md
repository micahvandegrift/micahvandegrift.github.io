# The Transition to the 1990s

*Topic 8 of the course archive.*

## Overview

The move from the 1980s to the 1990s was not only an ideological shift, with the popularity of the underground, but also a musical shift. Ideology is the ideas and manner of thinking characteristic of a group, social class or individual: an organized collection of abstract ideas, a comprehensive vision of a group. The ideology of the underground is wrapped up in its conceptions and definitions of indie and DIY.

To describe the ideological shift: in the first three quarters of the twentieth century it was easy to sort music into a stylistic core (whatever was popular and easily accessible: Tin Pan Alley, jazz, disco) and a periphery (under the radar, different from the norm: blues, folk, heavy metal). The rise of the 1980s underground, and the transitional genres that carried it into the 1990s, complicate that model considerably. There is no longer a center and a periphery, but a hodgepodge of musics influencing and being influenced by one another.

The transitional genres are second-wave punk, metal, and post-hardcore: emocore, lo-fi and indie. This topic follows them through Hüsker Dü and Rites of Spring in the 1980s, the cultural landscape of the 1990s, and 1994, the year indie rock's shape came into view.

## Lo-fi and cassette culture

Lo-fi comes from "low fidelity," the quality of the music in its output. It is most often associated with tapes and analog recording methods. As an outgrowth of the underground press and indie label distribution, cassette culture was born, and sharing mixtapes became a way of spreading music across the landscape. K Records and Beat Happening were seminal in this movement.

## Indie as college rock: Hüsker Dü

Hüsker Dü, from Saint Paul, Minnesota, is Danish and Norwegian for "Do you remember?" The members were Bob Mould, Grant Hart and Greg Norton. Their logo has a circle for the band, three lines for the members and an intersecting line for the common train of thought.

Azerrad's account, as I read it in class, makes several points:

* They had a huge role in convincing the underground that melody and punk rock were not antithetical (p. 159).
* They became a key link between hardcore and the more melodic, accessible music that would eventually be termed "college rock."
* Their eventual move to a major label sent shock waves through the indie community. R.E.M. and U2 had paved the way, but no one from the community had ever made the leap.
* They, along with a few other bands, created the mainstream market for the commercialization of "college rock."
* Their sound was folk rock played at light speed, with distortion and extreme volume.
* They began to disavow the preachy aspects of hardcore: "We want to pose questions, not answers" (Bob Mould, p. 168).

*Zen Arcade* was released in July 1984 on SST. It is a concept album, the ultimate classic-rock idea, a story about a kid who runs away, deals with drugs and real life, and returns home. "Something I Learned Today" is a simple refutation of hardcore orthodoxy. It is loud and fast, but its use of melody and chord structures is very un-hardcore. The zine *Hard Times* put it this way: the story "encapsulates the wreckage of our era, betrayal, alienation, pain, egotism, emotional suffocation and isolation" (p. 182). It is similar to hardcore in its negative view of the world, but pushed to an extreme limit of emotional connectedness to these issues.

*Zen Arcade* began the trend of opening this music up to a new group of people: college students, interested in the lyrics, reading between the lines. It garnered tons of critical acclaim, many best-of lists, and several of its songs were included in lists of the top 500 songs of the 1980s. It represents the sound of an entire musical movement growing up.

Hüsker Dü was also one of the first key underground bands to make the jump to a major label, releasing *Candy Apple Grey* on Warner Bros. in 1986. The Replacements, who had also come up on an independent label, signed with Sire the year before. They are claimed as an influence on bands from Green Day to the Pixies.

## Emocore: Rites of Spring

Emocore means emotional hardcore. There are numerous theories about where the term came from. Some say it was a derogatory term used at an Embrace show; others say Rites of Spring used it in an interview with *Flipside*.

It was the musical reaction against the violence and close-mindedness of the DC hardcore scene. In DC at this time, the scene was beginning to fracture as hardcore waned, and the core members of the scene had moved out, grown up or become disenchanted with the way things were. Out of these feelings came the idea of Revolution Summer, which we read about in *Dance of Days*.

What did it sound like? The guitars carried it: rock-leaning music at a moderate tempo, with two guitarists strumming distorted chords mostly in unison and an occasional catchy riff on top, and a singer who sounded raw and emotional rather than posed and smooth like a pop vocalist. This became known as the classic "D.C. sound," and the octave chords heard in later "emo" belong to the same inheritance. Later bands brought in more pop: catchier riffs, pop song structures, and cleaner, higher singing with less yelling and strain. Jawbreaker's "Chesterfield King" shows the change.

## The cultural landscape of the 1990s

If the 1980s was the "Me Generation," the 1990s was the "youth generation." Youth culture, and everything involved in it, was the staple of 1990s cultural history.

**Generation X and the slackers.** In the early 1990s the media portrayed Generation X as flannel-wearing, alienated, overeducated, underachieving slackers with body piercings, who drank franchise-store coffee and worked at McJobs. There was some truth in this, but much of it was stereotype. The term "Generation X" was popularized by Douglas Coupland's novel *Generation X: Tales for an Accelerated Culture* (1991), whose characters were born in the late 1950s and early 1960s and felt little connection to the cultural icons of the baby boom. In Coupland's usage, the X stood for an unknown variable, a generation that did not want to be defined.

**Fashion.** Anti-fashion in the first half of the decade, with an emphasis on the individual and personal taste: tattoos and piercings, grunge, hip hop, retro. Films like *Clerks*, *Mallrats* and *Reality Bites* encouraged it.

**Technology.** The technology boom of the 1980s continued. The internet connected the world in new ways. By 1994 three million people were online, and by 1998 it was 100 million. MTV continued as a forerunner of youth culture with *The Real World* in 1992. The X Games were established in 1995, and extreme sports gained popularity.

**Music.** While rock held popular attention for most of the decade (grunge, alternative, nu metal), hip hop culture was also a major force. Many 1990s slang words and phrases were related to hip hop culture ("dawg," "phat," "da bomb"). Tupac Shakur and the Notorious B.I.G. were caught up in the public East Coast-West Coast rivalry, and both were killed in shootings, Shakur in September 1996 and Christopher Wallace (B.I.G.) in March 1997. The Fugees, Boyz II Men and TLC were huge throughout the decade.

**Society and politics.** Racial issues were brought into the public eye by the L.A. riots, the O.J. Simpson murder case and the Million Man March, and multiculturalism became a concern of education, politics and global relations. With the collapse of the USSR, the United States was the world's sole superpower. In 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait, which led to the Gulf War in early 1991, and the U.S. later got involved in Somalia (1992-94), Haiti (1994), Bosnia (1995) and Yugoslavia (1999). Bill Clinton's two terms brought Democratic concerns back to the White House, and he presided over one of the longest peacetime economic expansions in American history, with federal budget surpluses at the end of the decade. The decade also saw the World Trade Center bombing (1993), Oklahoma City (1995) and Columbine (1999).

**Entertainment.** Throughout American music history, the margins have been the progressors of music. In the 1990s there was an ironic twist: making the notion of outsider, alternative or marginal music a means of promoting music to a mass audience searching for a sense of authenticity. The tension between commercialism and authenticity can be said to be the main thought process of the underground in this period. The mainstream was Hootie and the Blowfish, Alanis Morissette, Celine Dion, Garth Brooks and the Spice Girls; the movies, *Schindler's List* (1993), *Forrest Gump* (1994) and *Titanic* (1997), with *Pulp Fiction* (1994) as the indie film. Adult-oriented cartoons arrived (*The Simpsons*, *Family Guy*, *Beavis and Butt-Head*), *Seinfeld* and *Friends* had large followings, and TV pushed boundaries on sexual orientation (*Ellen*, *Will and Grace*), nudity, language, crude behavior, violence and political incorrectness.

### What this meant for the underground

In the epilogue to Azerrad's book, he states that Nirvana's *Nevermind* (September 1991) synthesized the music the industry had been ignoring for most of the 1980s, from bands like Black Flag, Hüsker Dü, Dinosaur Jr., the Pixies and the Melvins. "*Nevermind* made the sound palatable to the mainstream with strong melodies and slick production" (p. 494). College radio became a mining ground thanks to the success of R.E.M. and U2. Guy Picciotto: "The conversations changed after 1991. Before people talked about ideas and music. And then after that people talked about money and deals" (p. 495).

Three things mark the change: **visibility**, active interaction with the mainstream; **agency**, the underground's ability to be effective in social, cultural and political America; and **proliferation** of the fan and member base: "everyone was doing it themselves, and a lot of it was mediocre." Indie and underground moved from an idea to a genre.

## 1994

1994 represents the shifts of the 1990s very well. A boiling point was reached, and the output of the underground reflected it. In the 1980s the underground had a semi-unified feel across the board; in the 1990s there was a whole slew of new concerns and new ways of doing DIY. The albums released in 1994 had profound effects, then and now.

* **Green Day, *Dookie*:** released February 1 on Reprise Records. It rose to number 2 on the Billboard 200 and raised serious questions in the underground about selling out. It won the Grammy for Best Alternative Music Album in 1995.
* **Weezer, the Blue Album:** released May 10 on DGC Records. Several of its videos, directed by Spike Jonze, won awards, and "Buddy Holly" won four MTV Video Music Awards in 1995. The album is often cited as an influence on later emo. The band calls its influences KISS, Nirvana and the Pixies, showing how 1990s music was beginning to recognize influence from various generations of music.
* Limp Bizkit formed. Kurt Cobain died in April; his body was found on April 8.

Two subgenres to focus on: second-wave punk and lo-fi. Lo-fi was fully in fruition in 1994. It is associated with earlier bands like Beat Happening, Sebadoh (founded by Lou Barlow while he was still in Dinosaur Jr.) and cassette culture (K Records). Lo-fi artists often work with old, cheap or poor equipment, partly because they cannot afford better, but also because that gear sounds authentic to listeners who have spent years hearing demos, bootlegs, field recordings and plainly produced older pop records. Lo-fi grew up alongside the extreme slickness and polish of 1980s multitrack pop recording.

Some call lo-fi "true indie rock." Meanwhile "alternative" became commercialized: mainstream success brought in major-label money and manufactured, formulaic acts, and the word moved away from its original counter-cultural sense to mean alternative music that sold well, while "indie rock" came to mean the bands and genres that stayed underground. Lo-fi is also said to have been influenced by The Smiths.

### Guided by Voices

Guided by Voices formed in Dayton, Ohio, in 1983, and their principal songwriter is Robert Pollard. Their influences include psychedelic rock, prog rock, punk and post-punk. They began to capitalize on the success of college radio bands like R.E.M., and in 1993 started to get attention from the press, including *Spin*. In 1994 they released *Bee Thousand* on Scat Records, with distribution through Matador, a major player in 1990s indie circles. They put on a great live show, playing up their chops as a show band after years of experience.

*Bee Thousand* was the culmination of early lo-fi, mixed with early emocore, combined with the melodic structures of pop and the attitude of grunge, transplanted in the Midwest. It also signifies an important shift from the East Coast and West Coast polarization of the scene to the rise of middle America through indie, emo and late-1990s progressive indie emo.

### Pavement

Pavement formed in 1989 in Stockton, California; the primary songwriter is Stephen Malkmus. *Crooked Rain, Crooked Rain* was released on February 14 (1994) on Matador Records. Because of lineup changes and access to a studio, its sound was a jump from the lo-fi of their 1992 release, *Slanted and Enchanted*. The album gained some critical acclaim but did more to expand their cult fan base. It peaked at number 121 on the Billboard 200, and "Cut Your Hair" became a Top Ten hit on the alternative charts and is said to have been an MTV hit. On "Cut Your Hair," Malkmus mocks the importance of image in the music business, with the haircut as the emblem, and the song can be heard as a jab at the fad for alternative music and at Nirvana imitators.

Both bands gained some commercial success but were spurred on mostly by the cult following they developed.
