# Religion in the Underground

## Overview

Is the morality and ethics of underground music easily connected to religious ideas? Why is this a theme in a course on underground music? Religion is a part of American culture. Current groups are addressing similar issues. And often the language and ideals of the underground are religious in tone: believe, authenticity, morality, truth, suffering, persecution.

The most obvious link to religious ideas is the connection to straight edge, a moral lifestyle. This lesson follows that thread into Krishnacore, then into Christian punk and hardcore, and closes with Pedro the Lion, a band that gives voice to the questions its songwriter has about the Christian upbringing that shaped him.

## Discussion questions

* How is spirituality made a problem for groups that have the label "Christian" attached to them without their permission? (Mutemath, Pedro the Lion.)
* How does Pedro the Lion fit into the underground? Musically and aesthetically it fits our model, but the content is a little different.
* Does spiritual content in lyrics change the authority of a work of art?
* The questions Pedro raises (capitalism, image, anti-authority) fall in line with many other underground artists. Is it important to include the process of belief in discussing this subculture? Why?
* There is a trend in softer folk that is vaguely spiritual. Bands like Iron and Wine use the language and images of religion to tell stories. How much of this storytelling, including religious themes, is part of a new movement in the underground toward abstracting the message and making it more artistic? Can we point to it as a trend?

## Krishnacore

Hare Krishna is a religion in which, by chanting the mantra, you can reach a higher state of life. It is associated with the hippies and the Beatles. As one of its proponents puts it, "Krishna consciousness is not an artificial imposition on the mind; this consciousness is the original energy of the living entity."

Krishna consciousness was introduced to the straight edge subculture by Larry Pugliese in New York City in the early 1980s. He established a house, free food distribution, clothing drives and concerts for punks living on the Lower East Side. Bands like the Cro-Mags and Youth of Today came out of this group, including Ray Cappo and John Porcelly ("Porcell") of Youth of Today and later Shelter, and Harley Flanagan and John Joseph of the Cro-Mags.

Krishnacore is the name for hardcore played by people in the scene who were connected to the Hare Krishna movement. Its roots were in the early and mid-1980s, in New York bands such as Cause for Alarm and the Cro-Mags (although Krishnacore itself did not exist yet). It grew out of straight edge and established itself as a sect inside hardcore in the early 1990s, with Shelter and 108.

John Joseph of the Cro-Mags met Bad Brains and was inspired by their Rastafarianism, then met some Krishnas in New York. Comparing the Cro-Mags to other Krishna-conscious bands, he said in a 1995 *Spin* article, "It wasn't like we were up on stage in dhotis and chanting. It was all-out assault." In Vedic philosophy there are different kinds of people, he says; not everyone is meant to be a monk, and there were the warriors. He would chant and meditate before going onstage and get into a warrior mentality.

**Youth of Today** (1985-1990) recorded for Positive Force Records and Revelation Records. They were part of the second wave of straight edge, known for positive thinking, melodic hardcore and a New York base. Similar bands: Gorilla Biscuits, Bold and Sick of It All. Ray Cappo is seen as one of the first to move from straight edge to full-on Hare Krishna. In 1989 *Maximum Rocknroll* put him on the cover with the title "Ray Cappo and the Krishnas." The article, written by Tim Yohannan, tried to make the movement look negative, but Ray's responses inspired many to look into it, including Norman Brannon (also known as Norm Arenas), of the zine *Anti-Matter* and the band Texas Is the Reason. Cappo later went to India and became a priest.

Straight edge kids were already used to saying no to many pleasures of the flesh, which made it easier to take on the four regulative principles that devotees follow: no meat, fish or eggs, no intoxication, no illicit sex and no gambling. Where most people would have to make a huge leap away from materialistic habits, many of these young people found it a relief to give those habits up for the satisfactions of spiritual practices such as mantra meditation. The major scenes were Brooklyn, Philadelphia and Washington, DC.

**Shelter** (formed around 1990-91) has released about ten albums, on Equal Vision, Revelation, Roadrunner and Victory, among other labels. The members were Porcell and Ray Cappo. Their album *Perfection of Desire* (1990), written by Ray and recorded with friends, helped create the new face of Krishnacore. Norman Brannon (Norm Arenas) played guitar with them for a time in the early 1990s. Porcell told *Spin* in 1995, "We do it for our own purification… There's a method to changing your consciousness." He compares Shelter's thunderous growl to the sacred bhajans the devotees sing for hours in the temple: onstage, he is meditating on the lyrics. The article's author describes the devotees he talked to as "not dogmatic robots but thoughtful people negotiating the conflicting demands" of religious practice and the modern world.

**Equal Vision Records** was founded by Ray Cappo and Vic DiCara (of the Orange County band Inside Out, which featured Zack de la Rocha) in the Philadelphia temple, to put out Krishnacore records. They sold the label to Steve Reddy in 1992. Bands have included Earth Crisis, Shelter and Coheed and Cambria. **Revelation Records** was founded in 1987 by Jordan Cooper and Ray Cappo, originally to put out Warzone's *Lower East Side Crew* 7-inch. It also put out Shai Hulud, and later moved to California.

## Christian punk and hardcore

In Andrew Beaujon's words, the Christian underground "is perched precariously between a subculture that's never been entirely comfortable with what used to be seen as the devil's music and a mainstream that's never been entirely comfortable with what it sees as a recruiting tool for the other side."

The subculture can be pointed to as beginning with Doug Van Pelt's fanzine *Heaven's Metal* in 1985, which combined his three loves: Jesus, hard rock and music journalism. In the 1990s he changed the name to *HM: Hard Music Magazine* and began to include punk, alternative and other new hard genres.

**Tooth and Nail** was founded in 1993 by Brandon Ebel "as a means to give independent artists a forum to creatively express positivity through music." It is an independent label whose artists are often not accepted in the mainstream Christian market. Is this the same split that happens in other genres we have discussed? Is it a subculture within a subculture? Fifty percent of the label's sales are outside the Christian market. MXPX's *Pokinatcha* (1994) was, for many Christian teens who were not allowed to listen to Blink-182 or Green Day, an introduction to the subculture of punk as a whole. They helped create a category that perplexed many (Christian, mainstream, underground), and they said they were Christians in a band, not a Christian band. The album sold a hundred thousand copies.

### Spirit-filled hardcore

This is a brand of hardcore typified by bold, faith-based lyrics, a typical hardcore aesthetic and often a straight edge mentality. It saw its rise and fall in the 1990s.

**Strongarm**, from Pompano Beach, Florida (1993-1998), released two EPs and two albums on Tooth and Nail, and are called the godfathers of Christian hardcore. They shared members with Shai Hulud, and after they broke up most of the members went on to form Further Seems Forever, whose singer, Chris Carrabba, then went on to Dashboard Confessional. They sang blatantly about faith and dealt with the issues of life through their Christianity. Their purpose, as stated at most if not all of their shows, was to be missionaries into the underground. Because the underground was for the most part built on ideas of tolerance and respect, Strongarm was not compartmentalized to Christian audiences, and fan reviews suggest their religious lyrics mattered to fans from all walks of life.

**Unashamed** (1994-1996) built their bold faith-based lyrics around ideas directly lifted from the Bible and conservative morality, instead of the usual punk themes of teen angst, anger and pain. Their music was in the vein of East Coast hardcore and metalcore. "Who's to Blame" is similar in form to Fugazi's "Suggestion."

### Anti-religion

Omega Man and Modest Mouse are the examples here of bands that come at religion from the opposite direction.

## Pedro the Lion

David Bazan, who is Pedro the Lion, was raised Christian, with a father who was a music minister, and was not allowed to listen to secular music until high school. He was inspired by Nirvana and Fugazi: "They're sort of the general-market equivalent of what I loved about Christianity growing up… There was this sense of authenticity and this really strong ethical sense about what they were doing. You felt it in their music, you felt it in everything they were doing." He drummed in an early band, The Guilty (later Coolidge), with Damien Jurado.

Pedro the Lion formed in Seattle in 1995 to emulate slowed-down indie bands like Sebadoh, Bedhead and Low. Bazan calls his style "slow-core." They released an EP, *Whole*, in 1997 on Tooth and Nail, and Bazan played most of the instruments on the first two records, both released on Jade Tree. The lineup changed constantly, and Tim Walsh was the only other semi-regular. They put out four albums and ended their career in 2006; the most notable is *Control* (2002).

The importance of Pedro is that it reflects and gives voice to Bazan's questions about his Christian upbringing, which is generally discouraged in the Christian subculture. At the time he was struggling to define himself outside Christian culture. This is a common theme for many bands that come out of the subgenre: they want to be recognized for their music, not their faith. That happens a lot in the underground, where a high premium is placed on musicality. Of *Whole*, he says, "The band has basically been a document of the tension between living by my own code and being a good Christian." The songs on *Whole* include "Nothing," "Lullaby" and "Secret of the Easy Yoke."

The 1999 EP *The Only Reason I Feel Secure* includes "Letter from a Concerned Follower." The album *It's Hard to Find a Friend* began to be played on the University of Washington radio station, which allowed Pedro the Lion to play shows outside the church circuit. In reviews he is often called a cynic.

With *Control* (2002), he began to deliberately critique the American system of capitalism and Christian politics. He began to read liberation theology and hard-left literature like Noam Chomsky. Liberation theology "helped me understand my faith. Not what Christianity is but what Jesus talked about. It's very much in line with these things that I am starting to think and feel about politics. About the belief in the unfair nature of corporate America, the political process, and things like that." Here he equates the structure of the American church with a corporation, and the power it holds over those it influences, which is radically different from other bands coming out of the Christian tradition.

Despite his radical stance on America and religion, he is still very popular in the Christian subculture, and he is a divisive figure among his fans. Some swear he is preaching true Christianity, being Christlike, and some cannot get over the fact that he swears and drinks. He always takes questions from the audience. At concerts he is often asked if he is still a Christian, and he usually answers no. Andrew Beaujon asked him why: "I'd rather there just be a misunderstanding because it is not necessary for strangers to know exactly how I feel." He is quite the opposite of the typical Christian model of evangelizing.
