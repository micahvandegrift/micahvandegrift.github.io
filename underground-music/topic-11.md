# Gender in the Underground

## Overview

As culture changed and evolved in the 1990s, gender issues peaked in influence. Spurred by the rise of identity politics, mixed with social concerns for freedoms and rights, a new generation of feminists was born, focused on women's rights, gay rights and abortion rights. It happened in mainstream America as well as in the underground, and perhaps more radically and influentially in the underground.

Two major movements came out of punk: riot grrrl and queercore. These gender movements matter because punk and the underground were being taken into cultural movements that were important to the world, and because people outside punk began to realize that DIY and the infrastructure of punk, zines and record labels, were useful for spreading important ideas. The key point: both movements were founded on, and spread mostly through, written media. Zines.

## Riot grrrl

Riot grrrl did not just happen out of nowhere. The movement was a culmination and continuation of other trends that had been going on before: women in punk, and in the early hardcore and metal scenes. Third-wave feminism was aimed at taking feminism out of intellectualism and academia and making it relevant to real women, and riot grrrl was an offshoot of it, with roots in the music, the DIY ethos and the indie spirit of the underground. As Amy Schroeder of *Venus Zine* put it, "the riot grrrl movement was based on a feminist and DIY philosophy that women can empower themselves by taking action politically and artistically." Amy Spencer, in *DIY: The Rise of Lo-Fi Culture*, writes that the riot grrrls "redefined feminism for the 90s and recognized each other as manufacturers of culture as opposed to participants in a culture they were forced to accept."

The movement was centered on the Pacific Northwest and Washington, DC, and mostly happened in the early 1990s. The major figures include Kathleen Hanna (Bikini Kill, later Le Tigre) and Allison Wolfe (Bratmobile), and the labels Kill Rock Stars (Slim Moon) and K Records in Olympia, Washington, put out many of the bands. It was focused around music, but for many the main concern was spreading the ideals of feminism, through workshops, support groups, festivals and zines. Among the most influential zines were *Riot Grrrl* (compiled in 1991 by Kathleen Hanna, Molly Neuman, Allison Wolfe and Jen Smith), *Girl Germs* (Neuman and Wolfe) and *Bikini Kill* (Hanna, Tobi Vail and Kathi Wilcox); *Revolution Girl Style Now!* was Bikini Kill's 1991 demo cassette.

From August 20 to 25, 1991, K Records put on the International Pop Underground Convention, with a first night, Girl Night, devoted to female bands. In 1992, feeling misrepresented by the mainstream media (including bands like Hole being lumped in with riot grrrl), key players, beginning with Kathleen Hanna, called for a year of non-communication with the press. This created a vacuum that made the media more than interested, to the point of exploitation, and their slogan "Girl Power," which Bikini Kill had used since 1991, was taken up by the Spice Girls in 1996. Riot grrrl burned out quickly as a force but affected change nonetheless, carrying on in bands like Le Tigre (Kathleen Hanna) and Sleater-Kinney (Carrie Brownstein), and in many DIY activist groups since, such as the Radical Cheerleaders.

## Queercore

Queercore is a subgenre of punk music but also a cultural movement for a homosexual voice in the punk community. It, too, did not come out of nowhere: there were gay members of the punk world before queercore. It is a culmination, and a name that grew out of zine culture.

The early queer zines include *JDs*, founded by Bruce LaBruce (gay) and G.B. Jones (lesbian) in Toronto in 1985. They were put out by the gay scene for being punk and on the outskirts of the punk scene for being queer, so they "turned to the traditionally punk medium of the zine to express their views… to reach others with similar attitudes in order to network, share ideas, and develop an alternative culture, which would then blend queer and hardcore identities" (Spencer, p. 41). In February 1989, LaBruce and Jones wrote an article for *Maximum Rocknroll* as a call for queer punks to unite. Other zines included *Homocore* (Tom Jennings, San Francisco), *Holy Titclamps* (Larry-bob, Minneapolis) and *Outpunk* (Matt Wobensmith, San Francisco).

This loosely connected sub-subculture shared ideas through the mail and met for the first time at SPEW, a national queer zine gathering, in May 1991 in Chicago. The bands include Pansy Division, Team Dresch and Fifth Column. Pansy Division toured with Green Day in 1994, took queercore into the mainstream and inspired many to get into the music as well as the subculture. For many, queercore was a political movement more than a musical one.
