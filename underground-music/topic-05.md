# Politics and Communication

## Overview

America is a country obsessed with its politics, and for good reason: democracy gives us the opportunity to choose. This topic asks how that choice filters down through different levels of society, and what it meant for the underground in the 1980s. The important shift for this subculture, again a reflection of the 1960s counterculture, is the emphasis on cultural change that would in turn change the political structures of the nation. The mere formation of an "underground" in opposition to a "mainstream" is a culturally progressive movement that affected the political ideas of everyone in it. The "damn the man" attitude carries over into politics.

We look at two kinds of politics in hardcore punk. The first is the outward-facing, satirical politics of the Dead Kennedys and their singer, Jello Biafra. The second is the "personal politics" of Minor Threat, and the straight edge movement that grew out of their songs.

## Politics in the early underground

The political context of the 1980s is described in Topic 1: the Misery Index, Reagan, the New Conservatism, Reaganomics and the Yuppies.

In the punk subculture there are often stereotypical associations of political leanings that may or may not be accurate: anarchist (punk of the 1970s), left wing, radical. These labels fit some people and are complicated to define for others, especially in the political world after 1980. What "left wing" and "right wing" mean depends on the country and the period. In broad terms, the right is associated with social and moral conservatism, order, tradition and religion, and the left with redistributing wealth, equality and secularism.

The radicals in the underground ran the gamut of politicization, from anarchy to patriotism. They were most often associated with left-wing politics: opposition to war and support for equality, racial and gender. The 1980s underground took up the process of writing a history of rebellion through action, not just words. It took the best parts of the 1960s and combined them with the attitude of 1970s punk.

Negation gave way to dissent. Negation, a cultural concept, informed a lifestyle of dissent, which is a political ideology, and it gave the underrepresented a voice.

## The Dead Kennedys

The Dead Kennedys formed in San Francisco in 1978: East Bay Ray, Jello Biafra, Klaus Flouride, the drummer Ted, and the rhythm guitarist 6025, whose real name was Carlos Cadona. Their technique is the same as Stephen Colbert's. Satire and a sarcastic tone run through many of their songs, along with various literary references.

Their first single was "California Über Alles." The song is sung in the voice of California governor Jerry Brown and imagines a hippie-fascist America in which a secret police goes after people who are not cool. It illustrates Biafra's concern with the dilution of the radical tendencies of the 1960s by "yuppies" and their representatives, such as, one presumes, Brown. The Alternative Tentacles label was founded in 1979 to put out this single.

The second single, a 7-inch released in May 1980, was "Holiday in Cambodia." Written not long after the Khmer Rouge years, it mocks comfortable, self-satisfied young Westerners by setting their lives against life in Pol Pot's Cambodia.

Their first album, *Fresh Fruit for Rotting Vegetables* (1980), is a staple of American punk and a swan song for political punk. It was lumped in with hardcore, but the music itself isn't similar to hardcore. It includes "Kill the Poor," "Let's Lynch the Landlord" and "California Über Alles."

Next came the EP *In God We Trust, Inc.* (1981), with artwork by Winston Smith. By now the group had become a de facto political force, pitting itself against rising elements of American social and political life such as the religious right, Ronald Reagan and the idle rich. Songs include "Moral Majority" and "We've Got a Bigger Problem Now."

With *Frankenchrist* (1985), the band got into legal trouble over a poster packaged with the album, a reproduction of an H.R. Giger painting. This was the height of their conflict with the forces they had opposed for so long, among them the PMRC, the Parents Music Resource Center associated with Tipper Gore. *Bedtime for Democracy* followed in 1986, and the band broke up after it. Cover art was always an important part of the Dead Kennedys' approach to the consumer.

### Jello Biafra

Biafra, the band's lead singer, ran for mayor of San Francisco in fall 1979. As the Sinker collection puts it: "The DK's were instrumental in providing American punk with a leftist political philosophy that transcended the nihilism, ignorance, and stupidity of the early hardcore scene… DK's tried to encourage people to think for themselves by making it safe, however uncomfortable, to question the limits of countercultural politics and anti-establishment behavior."

### Skinheads

Very few people embraced the skinhead style without being affected by its politics. For most of these kids it was shock value, and it often reflected youthful anti-socialist inclinations rather than a coherent racialist doctrine. There were Nazi skins and anti-Nazi skins, right-wing skins and left-wing skins. Every action requires an equal and opposite reaction: the politicization of hardcore polarized it, with the extreme left pulling against the extreme right, and may have been its undoing.

## Personal politics: Minor Threat and straight edge

Minor Threat epitomized one of hardcore's major strengths: it was underground music by, for and about independent-minded kids. They were really inspired by Ted Nugent, who didn't smoke, drink or do drugs.

### The Teen Idles

Ian MacKaye met Jeff Nelson after Nelson set off a pipe bomb outside their school. They saw the Cramps in January 1979. Before that came White Boy, a local father-and-son band that played proto-punk songs. The Teen Idles were Ian, Jeff, Geordie Grindle and Nathan Strejcek. The themes of their music were what was happening around them: high school and friendships.

They did two demo sessions, in February and April 1980, and in August 1980 took a bus to California to play. Their last show was on November 6, 1980, at the 9:30 Club. The X's on their hands came from the California trip, where the club Mabuhay Gardens marked the underage band members' hands, and the band later proposed a similar arrangement to clubs back home, to show they were underage. It became the mark of straight edge. Their *Minor Disturbance* 7-inch EP was released in December 1980 as Dischord #1. They sent it to Tesco Vee at the *Touch and Go* fanzine, and "Get Up and Go" was number one for a few weeks on the *Maximum Rocknroll* radio show.

### Minor Threat

Minor Threat were formed out of the ashes of the Teen Idles: Ian, Jeff, Lyle Preslar and Brian Baker. Their first show was on December 17, 1980, opening for Bad Brains. MacKaye says, "I was 19 and about to leave the ranks of teenagers and I was furious." They debuted with a self-titled 7-inch EP, Dischord #3, recorded in May 1981 and released in June.

Their themes were less about outside politics and more about personal politics. These were middle-class and upper-middle-class kids, not much worried about "the system," but concerned with how politics affected their lifestyles. Ian: "I didn't know enough about politics to really sing about them. And I didn't know enough about the world to really sing about it. But I knew enough about MY world to sing about it." And: "Free choice and independent thinking were the real point of straight edge. FLEX YOUR HEAD!" Controlling things, not having them control you.

In August 1981 they toured with Youth Brigade and recorded *In My Eyes*, a four-song EP. At Christmas 1981 they played what was meant to be their final gig, because Preslar was moving away. They reformed in April 1982, with Baker becoming the second guitarist and Steve Hansgen taking over on bass. They recorded *Out of Step* in January 1983, and in spring 1983 did a 49-day, 33-show tour of the U.S. and Canada.

By the summer of 1983, regional pride in DC had become territorial, a matter of turf. The band started to come apart, not only because of the breakdown of their scene but also because of the feelings of the band members toward Ian. They felt he was too preachy and was losing sight of fun. The rest of the band was getting into U2 and also wanted to go more mainstream, in sound and ideals. They played their last show on September 23, 1983, and recorded the *Salad Days* single on December 14, 1983.

## Straight edge

Straight edge was one of the most influential social, cultural and political movements of the 1980s underground. It came out of two songs written and performed by Minor Threat: "Straight Edge" and "Out of Step."

The basic idea is abstinence from alcohol, tobacco and drugs. Sometimes it also includes caffeine, vegetarianism, veganism, and abstaining from promiscuity. The reasons for being edge are philosophical, social or religious, and often just to become an active member of the scene. Some people kept it as a personal ideal; others used it as a militaristic code of solidarity.

**Boston** is the example of the second kind. SSD and Al Barile formed the first legitimate hardcore crew there. Barile took what was going on in DC to the next step. The "Boston Crew" is named on the cover of SSD's album *The Kids Will Have Their Say* (1982), and the crew would travel with the band and represent the area. Boston was the most violent scene, with pig piling, and it is where straight edge got its militant edge: people would knock beers out of other people's hands. It was a closed-off, elitist scene.

Large straight edge scenes grew up in other cities: Salt Lake City (the Monster Crew), Louisville (the Courage Crew) and Tampa (the Chaos Crew).
