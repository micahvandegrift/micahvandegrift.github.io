# DIY, Indie and the Business of Music

## Overview

This lesson is about the principles of the underground: DIY, the difference between a subculture and a scene, and what it means to be independent. DIY (do it yourself) is one of the main dividers between punk and the underground. For the underground, the point was not to destroy the system but to create your own system by your own rules, and to do it well. Independence is at the core of the great debate of the subculture's growth over the past thirty years: indie versus major.

We close with Fugazi, the quintessential underground band, which lives by the principles of the underground.

## DIY

### A historic construct in three phases

DIY can be understood as a historic construct with three phases. First is **necessity**: you did it yourself because there was no other way. Second is **statement**, having to do with output: artistic, visual, musical, spoken word. Third is **aesthetic**: a lifestyle of being more authentic than something else.

### What DIY sounds like, looks like, and is

As music: the Teen Idles' "Get Up and Go" is an example, a song that shrugs off talent and theory in favor of getting up and playing. As an art form: the flyers, album covers and posters of the underground. As a lifestyle: going against the norm for the sake of purity.

### According to the authors

* Sinker's introduction calls DIY "the foundation that the entire culture is built upon."
* Azerrad (p. 6) calls it necessity more than statement.
* Steven Blush describes "a new definition of musical success: in non economic terms… objective-oriented, community based culture" (p. 275), and self-reliant, anti-corporate individuals becoming entrepreneurs.

### According to the artists

* "DIY was our version of punk." Mike Watt of the Minutemen (Azerrad, p. 77).
* "One aspect of DIY is that you really have to do it yourself. It's work!" Ian MacKaye (Sinker, p. 19).
* "The only venue left for DIY is the computer." Art Chantry (Sinker, p. 189).
* "I was expected to get involved… not all of it was music, a point that is often missed. What the scene really did was inspire and attract talented people of all stripes." Jack Rabid, editor of *The Big Takeover*.

### DIY as an ethic and as a culture

The DIY ethic means being self-reliant: doing a job yourself instead of paying someone else, and trusting ordinary people to learn more than they expect. The culture of DIY is a different thing: a political movement about taking responsibility for your own surroundings.

In punk, the ethic also reaches into daily life, as a way of not feeding institutions seen as exploitative. People run cars on biodiesel or vegetable oil, learn to fix bicycles, mend and alter their own clothes, plant gardens and dive into dumpsters. In a broad sense DIY is an economic model, and sharing skills is central to it.

DIY artists do not know what else to do, and often do not think of themselves as artists. For this subculture, DIY is the currency, the means, the end, your identity, and it provides a sense of authenticity. Truth, purity, authenticity.

## Subculture and scene

In the anthropologist's sense, culture is how people adapt to their surroundings: behavior that is learned and shared, not inherited, and handed down from one generation to the next. It is shared across sex, gender, age, class and occupation; it is learned (we all need to eat, but culture says how); it rests on symbols, language above all; and it is integrated, so its parts work together as a whole.

A subculture is a group whose behavior and beliefs, open or hidden, set it apart from the larger culture it belongs to. When a subculture sets itself systematically against the dominant culture, it can be called a counterculture. Sarah Thornton, drawing on Pierre Bourdieu, called the cultural knowledge and goods that raise a member's standing within a subculture "subcultural capital" (*Club Cultures*, 1995). Studying a subculture often means studying what clothes, music and other visible signs mean, and how outsiders read them.

A scene may be different from a subculture. It can be talked about as a collective of people, as an ideal, or as a fashion and marketing statement. A scene is made up of bands and music, a sense of community, a venue, a record store, and a zine or label.

## Independent, indie and the great debate

The meaning of "indie" has changed and morphed over time. In film, it meant working outside the Hollywood mainstream, caring more about art than entertainment, and aiming at viewers thought to have more refined tastes than the average moviegoer. Words often attached to it are offbeat, quirky, eccentric, unconventional, experimental and weird. It often deals with the underside of American culture: homosexuality, violence, religion seen in a different light, complex relationships, unhappy endings.

In plain terms, independent means working outside of some superstructure. In film that is Hollywood. In music it is the music business: corporate art, major labels, pop culture.

### Indie versus major

Music history can almost always be seen as growing out of independent minds. Rock music grew out of blues and jazz, and grassroots movements like folk in the sixties. American labels like Motown, Chess and Atlantic were all once indies (Azerrad, p. 5). Dischord created the model of independent record labels that many others followed. Quite a few bands moved from indies to majors and received a lot of criticism for it, including Sonic Youth, Hüsker Dü, The Replacements and more.

The mainstream plays the role of "the other." The concept of otherness is integral to understanding identities, because people construct roles for themselves in relation to an "other" as part of a fluid process of action and reaction. At stake is the integrity and quality of the music.

Art is a product of its time. Going indie, or "jamming econo," as the Minutemen put it, was a lash against the prosperity of the time. They were not saying they wanted to be poor, but that the excess was unnecessary.

Ian MacKaye and Steve Albini are giants of the underground subculture, and two of the most outspoken about their ideals of being, staying and living independent. The debate between indie and major is at the core of the subculture's growth over the last thirty years. Indie and DIY changed from a lifestyle to a mentality, from a business model to a moral and ethical model, from a philosophy to an ideology.

## Fugazi

Fugazi are the quintessential underground band that lives by the principles of the underground. The members are Ian MacKaye, Guy Picciotto, Joe Lally and Brendan Canty, all born between 1962 and 1966, which led to their appreciation of the political.

### Roots in Washington, DC

Fugazi formed out of various early-1980s DC bands, including Rites of Spring and Embrace. Revolution Summer in 1985 was the turning point. Rites of Spring's album was recorded in February 1985 at Inner Ear Studios, produced by Ian MacKaye and Michael Hampton, and the band announced the Punk Percussion Protest Against Apartheid, held on June 21 outside the South African Embassy (Azerrad, p. 381). MacKaye founded Embrace that summer with the former members of the defunct DC band Faith. Picciotto and MacKaye felt that the personal was political. These bands broadened the spectrum of what counted as good music beyond strict hardcore.

Fugazi are tied deeply to the DC scene. Ian MacKaye is half of Dischord Records. In late summer 1986 Ian, Joe Lally and Colin Sears began practicing as a three-piece. Sears, who was also drumming for Dag Nasty, left in January 1987 and was replaced by Brendan Canty. Fugazi's first show was on September 3, 1987. They were a three-piece at first; Guy Picciotto first sang backup, then became a full member, singing from 1988 and playing guitar from 1989. Their musical influences are said to be Ted Nugent, Jimi Hendrix, The Beatles and jazz. Their style is a mix of punk, hardcore, soul and noise, with rhythm sections using reggae and dub.

### Ethics

Fugazi are well known in indie circles for their ethics. For most of their career they kept ticket prices at five dollars, played all-ages shows, sold CDs for only ten dollars, and did not sell merchandise on tour. They will not give interviews to magazines that advertise cigarettes or alcohol. They are often concerned that such things will overshadow their music.

### The story

In January 1988 they made their first tour, mainly through Michigan, followed by a one-month U.S. tour that spring. They played Olympia, Washington, and founded a cross-national connection with that scene. During this tour they solidified their commitment to the band.

They released their first EP, *Fugazi*, in November 1988, recorded at Inner Ear Studios in June 1988 with Ted Niceley producing. The song "Suggestion" became one of their best known, about objectification from a female perspective. After that came a three-month European tour, staying in squats. The *Margin Walker* EP was recorded at the end of that tour at Southern Studios in London, in December 1988. They tracked a whole album but, tired from the tour, trimmed it to an EP, released in June 1989. The two records were later combined into *13 Songs* in September 1989. "And the Same," the second song on *Margin Walker*, has been described as a diatribe against racism and retrograde thinking.

After this Guy became the second guitar player, and the band began writing as a group, improvising and using new methods like texture, dissonance, feedback and harmonics. *Repeater* was recorded from July to September 1989 at Inner Ear, with Don Zientara engineering and Ted Niceley and the band producing, and released in April 1990, a classic post-punk album. Songs include "Repeater" and "Styrofoam." It is a mesh of various influences, very clean and organic, with a noticeable call and response taken from hip hop, which was growing at the time. It is practically a concept album about the notion that one can effect social change through what one buys ("Merchandise"). They toured in support of *Repeater* from March 1990 into 1991.

*Steady Diet of Nothing* (1991) was recorded in January and February with Don Zientara and released that July. After touring extensively, it took them time to work out how to record. It has some non-political songs, about genealogical heritage ("Latin Roots") and the disintegration of friendships ("Long Division"). It was released in the Year Punk Broke and gained Fugazi notoriety.

*In on the Kill Taker* (1993) reached number 153 on the Billboard 200 and has some of the band's most aggressive songs. Rolling Stone voted them best live band. *Red Medicine* (1995) is claimed by many to be their experimental album. On that tour they played worldwide, and by touching down in New Hampshire and Alabama completed all fifty U.S. states. *End Hits* came out in 1998. In 1999 came *Instrument*, a documentary and art film by a good friend, Jem Cohen. A soundtrack was also released that year, containing old demos and unreleased versions of songs, not any of the live material from the film. *The Argument* followed in 2001, with the EP *Furniture + 2 Songs* released the same day. Jerry Busher, who joined in 1998 as a second drummer and percussionist, also plays with the band.

### What they contributed to the underground aesthetic

* **Righteousness and integrity.** They pounded it into the underground: dogmatic in word and deed, all about being fair.
* **Music as community.** They held to the idea that music can be a vital, inspiring part of a community and can change society, with an emphasis on community as outside the mainstream, legitimate and self-supporting.
* **DIY made personal.** They personified DIY for many people, operating entirely outside the music business and often outside the underground as well.
* **A new sound.** Its roots are in hardcore sing-alongs, funk and reggae bass and drums, and American progressive originality.
* **A new relationship with the crowd.** A concert was no longer simple entertainment but an act to be done together. They defied the "laws" of space and created a new environment (Azerrad, p. 393).
* **Local politics.** They connected politically with their own community through the Positive Force group, co-founded by Mark Andersen, who wrote *Dance of Days* with Mark Jenkins.
* **A simple life.** Vegetarianism, recycling, DIY.
* **Staying indie.** They avoided major labels to keep full control of their output, and spread the wealth by choosing who to tour with and who to support.
